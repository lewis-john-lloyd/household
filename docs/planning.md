# Mortgage, retirement and pay history

The longer-range models.

## Mortgage

`mortgage.py` amortizes each loan in `loans` (principal, annual rate, term in months, origination date and the monthly escrow, mortgage insurance included, if the payment includes it) month by month from the first payment, due the second month after closing, applying `loan_events`: recorded payments (escrow comes off first; anything above the scheduled payment is extra principal), prepayments, recasts (a lump sum, then the same term at a lower payment) and rate changes. A month with no recorded payment takes the scheduled one. Compute writes the schedule (interest, principal, escrow, extra principal, balance) to the `mortgage` report beside what the household's own transactions categorized as mortgage paid that month. Loans arrive as a seed file (`loan_id,lender,originated,principal,rate,term_months,escrow`); events are typed into `loan_events`. A loan missing a field is skipped with a note.

## Retirement

`retirement.py` works out when the household can retire and how to draw its savings down, in today's dollars, with the taxes and the age rules that decide what a dollar in each kind of account is worth. Each adult's Social Security statement comes in through the inbox: on my Social Security, "Download your statement data" gives an XML file that `parsers/ssa_statement.py` reads into `ss_statements` (who, and their birth date), `ss_estimates` (the monthly benefit at each claiming age, 62 to 70) and `ss_earnings` (the earnings record), each statement's rows kept under its date and the latest used. The benefit is figured from the record the way the statement figures it (each year's earnings indexed to the national average wage index of two years ago, the highest 35 years, this year's bend points), so it can be figured again for any retirement year, with the zeros an earlier retirement leaves; a spouse gets the larger of their own benefit and half the other's. Until a person's statement is in, `birth_date:<person>` and `social_security_monthly:<person>` stand in.

The plan starts from today's balances by person and tax treatment (`investment_values`, the open lots' basis), the last 12 months' contributions (`investment_activity`, each row's money source deciding pre-tax or Roth), taxable savings (the last 12 months' income and vested shares less spending as the settings have it, taxes paid, and what went to IRAs and 529s), and the last 12 full months' spending by category, with each loan's payments ending at its payoff. Spending that stops or changes in retirement is a setting: `retirement_spending:<category or group>` takes an amount a year, or amounts from years (`0 from 2032; 9000 from 2040`); a name that isn't a category or group adds spending, and a blank value goes back to the last 12 months. Health cover before 65 (`retirement_health_pre65`) and Medicare after (Part B, IRMAA from the return two years before, and `retirement_health_supplement`) are added. Each retired year: Social Security, RMDs from the age the law sets by birth year (Uniform Lifetime Table), a 72(t) plan if the strategy has one, dividends and the HSA for health costs come in; the rest and the year's taxes come out of the accounts in the strategy's order, solved together; a conversion strategy fills ordinary income to the top of a bracket. Taxes follow the forms: the federal brackets and capital gains rates (the law in `taxdata.py`, held in today's dollars), the 65-and-over deductions, the taxable part of Social Security and the net investment income tax (their thresholds fixed in dollars, so shrinking with inflation), the 10% on money taken before 59 1/2 and Wisconsin's third of it, and Wisconsin's own (no tax on Social Security, 30% of long-term gains and 24,000 a person of retirement income excluded from 67). Before 59 1/2, money comes first from taxable accounts, Roth contributions and conversions five years old, the plan of an employer left in or after the year turning 55, and a 72(t) plan. Public law and its sources are in `retirelaw.py`.

Returns come three ways side by side: steady yearly returns (`retirement_cagrs`, before inflation), every historical sequence since 1928 starting in the retirement year, and Monte Carlo runs drawing whole years of stock, bond and inflation history at random (`markets.py`: Damodaran's returns, the BLS consumer price index), for a portfolio of `retirement_stock_share` stocks (default the allocation targets'). Compute tries every retirement year and takes the first whose Monte Carlo share of runs that never run short reaches `retirement_success_target` (0.9 unless set), or `retirement_year`; then compares every strategy there: withdrawal order (taxable, pre-tax, Roth; in proportion; pre-tax to the 12% bracket first), Roth conversions (none, or to the top of 12%, 22% or 24%), a 72(t) bridge, the spending rule (constant, Guyton-Klinger guardrails, variable percentage withdrawal) and the claiming ages, and takes `retirement_strategy` or the one that ends with the most after-tax wealth among the most successful. Reports gets `retirement_years`, `retirement_strategies`, `retirement_plan` (year by year for every strategy, with the Monte Carlo spread), `retirement_social_security`, `retirement_earnings` and `retirement_assumptions` (every number the plan starts from, where it came from, and the setting that changes it). The views' Retirement page has a part for each: Plan, Budget, Year by year, Strategies, Social Security and Assumptions. Budget (`retirement_budget` in Reports) lists every group and category with what it spent in the last 12 months and what the plan spends on it in its first year and in the retirement year; a category that looked like a one-off (most of its year in two months) is marked. Changing a line, leaving it out or adding one stages its `retirement_spending` setting like any other edit, and the next compute reruns the plan.

## Pay history

The ledger's `comp_events` tab is the history of pay changes in one place: each raise, promotion, new job or schedule change, with its effective date, person, employer, annual salary, band, rating, FTE and a note. It arrives as a seed file (`effective,person,employer,salary,band,rating,fte,note`) and is kept after that on the Jobs page of the views (a `job` edit; a row for the same date, person and employer corrects the earlier one), or by hand. Salary is the full-time salary a year and FTE the share of full time (blank is full time). The tax projection reads it for one thing: a job goes on until the person's next row for another employer (a raise at the same employer ends nothing), so a new job should get a row on the day it starts. Compute also sets the stubs beside it (`paycheck.py`, the `pay_check` table): each regular paycheck's base pay (salary, regular hours and paid time off) on a yearly footing by its pay period, against the row in force on the period's first day (salary times FTE). Two or more paychecks in a row with the same pay are a salary run, which disagrees when it is off by more than half a percent (a raise shows); the rest under one row, three or more, are hourly, and their median disagrees when off by more than 5% (hours vary). A job's first and last paychecks are left out as partial, and only paychecks since January 1 of last year are judged. The Jobs page shows a chart of each person's pay a year (salary times FTE) over the last twelve years: a step line colored by employer, the same employer the same color in both, a dashed ghost of the full-time salary above what is paid while part time, and a callout at each change (the new employer, a change of hours, or the raise, with raises close together told as one); hovering a change shows its detail; choosing a stretch of the line (by pointer or keyboard) shows its row under the chart, with what the paychecks say about it and a correction; and each chart's + button opens a pay change for that person in a dialog, filled in from the job in force. A status card counts the runs that disagree and opens the Jobs page, which lists them above the charts, each with the change its paychecks suggest (a run well under the salary suggests the same salary at a share of full time).
